My master’s thesis built a healthcare SDK and specified post-quantum resilience as a design goal. The work below is that goal taken seriously, plus tools built for problems I hit in my own work and released once they had earned it, each with the measurements and the failure cases that motivated it.

cbomctl — post-quantum policy conflict analysis

Reads a CycloneDX Cryptographic Bill of Materials and evaluates it against seven national post-quantum policies at once, reporting where their requirements contradict each other. Every rule cites the section or page it was read from, names who verified it, and records whether the source is a draft. The finding that came out of reading all seven: the authorities are not disagreeing about cryptography. Germany, France and the EU recommend hybrid key establishment; Australia recommends against it while granting the same premise; the NSA does not permit it on national security systems. It is a disagreement about which failure mode is likelier, not about the mathematics.

simframe — the iOS Simulator for coding agents

MCP server and CLI that gives an agent eyes, hands and memory on the iOS Simulator. A background loop keeps the newest frame warm, so a look at the screen costs about 20 ms instead of a blocking screenshot, and comes back as a numbered text map with tap points rather than an image. Measured rather than claimed: at the recorded median of n = 2 a step takes about 11.7 s against a human tester’s 1.95 s, and the engine’s own share is ~1.7 s — so making perception faster is nearly worthless, and n is the only variable worth touching. Accuracy is the half still unmeasured.

Writing

  • 155 tests failed, and none of them were broken

    A verification gate returned a detailed, specific, well-evidenced failure. It named the tests. It was wrong, and nothing in its output could have told me so.

  • Your gate is fine. Its reference is wrong.

    A broken automated check announces itself. A check whose baseline is corrupted keeps passing, keeps proving it ran, and quietly asks for less — and every summary statistic you have will say the run went well.

  • The term the latency model was missing

    A two-term model of per-step wall clock predicted the tool was at human speed. Running the benchmark suite end to end falsified it, and the third term reconciles two measurements that had looked contradictory.

  • A perfect score, and a veto

    I froze a decision rule before the tool had produced a single number, including one clause I thought was ceremony. The first result came back perfect on the metric and failed on the clause.
